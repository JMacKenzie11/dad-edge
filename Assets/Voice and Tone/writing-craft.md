# Writing Craft: Rules for Every Post

These are the craft principles that apply to every piece of writing produced for this publication. They work alongside the voice and style guide, the psychology reference, and the client profile. Where any rule here conflicts with a specific instruction in those files, those files win. Otherwise these rules apply without exception.

---

## Verbs

Use strong, specific verbs. Weak verbs are imprecise. Strong verbs do the work that adjectives and adverbs are trying to do badly. Instead of "he walked slowly," write "he trudged." Instead of "he was very angry," write "he wanted to put his fist through the wall." The verb carries the weight. Let it.

Question every use of "to be" and "to have." These are the weakest verbs in the language. They're static. They describe a state instead of an action. When you find "is," "was," "were," "has," or "have" in a sentence, ask whether a stronger verb would carry the same meaning with more force. Often it will.

Keep sentences active. Passive construction buries the actor and softens the impact. "He was blindsided by the loss" is weaker than "the loss blindsided him." Watch for words ending in -ed or -en preceded by a form of "to be," and watch for -ing endings that signal passive construction. Flip the sentence to make it active.

---

## Words

Prefer Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones. Short, plain, punchy words over long, abstract, fancy ones. "Help" over "assist." "Use" over "utilize." "Start" over "commence." "Show" over "demonstrate." Anglo-Saxon words hit harder and land faster. They're also the words these men actually use.

Drop "very" and other crutch words. "Very" almost never improves a sentence. Neither does "really," "quite," "rather," "somewhat," or "truly." These words are hedges that weaken what they're trying to strengthen. Cut them and make the underlying word do its job.

Don't repeat a distinctive word unless repetition is intentional and doing work. If a strong word appears in paragraph two, find a different word for the same concept in paragraph four. Repetition of ordinary words is fine. Repetition of distinctive ones draws attention to itself in the wrong way.

Twist clichés rather than using them straight. Men in this audience have heard every grief cliché there is and they've stopped hearing them. "Time heals all wounds" is invisible. Find the cliché underneath the thought and break it open, turn it sideways, or reject it directly. That's where the interesting writing lives.

---

## Sentences

Remove clutter. Short connector words, unnecessary pronouns, redundant prepositions, these accumulate and slow the reading down. Tighten every sentence by asking what can be removed without changing the meaning. Usually something can.

Be careful with "this" and "it." These pronouns are often unclear about what they're referring to. When a sentence starts with "This shows that..." the reader has to look backward to find what "this" means. Name the thing instead.

Use semicolons to link tightly related ideas. If two sentences are so connected that one grows directly from the other, a semicolon can hold them together without a full stop. Use this sparingly and only when the connection is genuinely tight.

Long sentences require conjunctions, rhythm, and a payoff at the end. A long sentence that wanders without landing anywhere is a long sentence that failed. If you're going to stretch a sentence out, make sure it's building toward something and that the final clause delivers. The length creates the tension. The ending releases it.

Interrupt long passages with a short sentence. After a long rolling sentence or a string of longer ones, a short sentence hits like a door closing. Use this deliberately. Not constantly.

Start sentences with a punch and end them stronger. The strongest position in a sentence is the end. Put the word or phrase that carries the most weight there. The beginning gets attention. The end gets remembered.

## Constructions that mark the writing as machine-made

These are sentence patterns that AI writing leans on so hard that readers now recognize them instantly. Each has a legitimate version and a reflexive version. The rules below draw that line.

The "It's not X, it's Y" construction. This is the single biggest tell in AI writing. The core frame of this publication uses it deliberately: "You're not broken. You're protected." That earns its place because it names the central reversal the whole body of work stands on, and it appears once, where it belongs. The violation is defaulting to it as a rhythm. If a draft contains two or more of these constructions, the writing is coasting on a template instead of doing the work. One per post, maximum, and only when the reversal is the actual point.

Anaphora, repeating the same opening word across consecutive sentences for drama. "You showed up. You did the work. You kept going." Used once in a post at a moment that earns it, this lands. Used as a default way to build intensity, it reads as manufactured emotion. The build-to-a-drop rhythm in the voice guide is the better tool for the same job.

The false dichotomy setup. "Some men shut down. Others explode. The truth is somewhere in between." This construction fakes insight by inventing two positions and then splitting the difference. If a post needs to contrast two responses, contrast two real, specific, observable behaviors and say something true about each one. Never resolve them into a mushy middle.

Question-as-transition. "So what does this actually mean for you?" placed before a section to fake momentum. Section headings in this publication are claims, and claims create their own momentum. If a transition needs a question to work, the section before it didn't finish its job.

Signposting and the summary sandwich. "First... Second... Finally..." in prose, telling the reader what the post will cover, covering it, and then telling him what it covered. This publication says things once. The structure should be invisible. The reader should feel the post building without being handed a map of it.

A note on threes. The rule of three in the Content section below stands. Three concrete behaviors, three beats building to a drop, three things he recognizes about himself. That's deliberate rhythm doing real work. The AI tell is different: reflexive triple adjectives and triple abstractions like "clear, honest, and direct" or "tired, disconnected, and numb" stacked in a single sentence because three feels complete. When three things appear together, each one must be specific enough to stand alone. If any of the three could be cut without losing meaning, cut it and let two, or one, do the job.

## Structure

Show first, then tell. Start with the concrete thing that happened, the specific moment, the observable behavior, and then, when the time is right, say what it means. Don't lead with the interpretation. Lead with the scene. Let the reader arrive at the meaning just before you name it.

Give the reader someone to root for. Every post is about a man the reader either is or recognizes. Put that man on the page early and specifically. Not "men who are grieving" but the man sitting in the driveway for twenty minutes before he goes inside. Specificity is what creates recognition.

Don't defend what you've written. Spend time revealing the truth, not protecting the argument. If a point needs two paragraphs of qualification before it can be made, the point is probably wrong or the approach is wrong. Say the thing directly and trust the reader to engage with it.

Don't filter. Don't point out that something is surprising, interesting, or important. If it's surprising, write it in a way that surprises. If it's important, write it in a way that lands with weight. Telling the reader how to feel about something is doing their job for them and it's condescending.

Trust the reader. These men are smart. They've been through something devastating and they're still standing. Don't over-explain. Don't repeat the point in different words to make sure it landed. Say it once, say it well, and move on. The reader will fill in what's needed.

Layer the sentences. Sentences carry more than information. They carry rhythm, tone, pace, and attitude. Every sentence is doing at least two jobs at once. The information job and the feeling job. Tend to both.

## Content

Write the hard stuff. Don't shy away from the dark corners of this subject. The men reading this have already lived through the dark. Writing that stays in safe territory won't reach them. The posts that land hardest are the ones that name things nobody else will name.

Use threes. For a series of things to land with force, you usually need three of them. Two feels incomplete. Four feels like a list. Three has weight and rhythm. "He goes to work, pays the bills, and shows up to the games" lands. "He goes to work and pays the bills" doesn't have the same force. See the note on threes in the machine-made constructions section: this rule covers concrete behaviors and narrative beats, not stacked adjectives.

---

## What these rules are not

These rules are not a formula. They're tools. Some of them will be more useful in some posts than others. Rule 34 from the original list applies here: a rule may be of universal use but need not be universally used. Use judgment. The goal is writing that reaches a man at 11pm who has nowhere else to turn and makes him feel like someone finally gets it. These rules exist to serve that goal, not the other way around.
