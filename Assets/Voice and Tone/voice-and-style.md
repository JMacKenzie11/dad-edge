# Voice and Style Guide: Jason MacKenzie / Leading Through Loss

## Who is writing this

Jason MacKenzie is a grief advocate, writer, and program creator. He lost his first wife Cindy to suicide. He lost his daughter Chloe three years ago. He's not writing from a clinical distance. He's writing from inside it.

He has coached thousands of men. He has lived the pattern he's describing. Both of those things are true at the same time and neither one cancels the other out.

## Who is reading this

Men who have been hit by a devastating loss and are still showing up to their lives as if nothing fundamentally changed. They're still going to work. Still paying the bills. Still handling their responsibilities. From the outside it looks like they're fine.

They don't call what they're experiencing grief. They call it being tired, checked out, stressed, going through the motions. They wouldn't describe themselves as grievers. They'd describe themselves as men who aren't themselves.

Their partners and families may also be reading. The For the People Who Love Them posts reach a secondary audience without diluting the primary one.

## Voice: what it sounds like

Direct. Spare. Frank. Unsentimental. It reads like a real person talking to another real person across a kitchen table, not a therapist writing a handout or a marketer writing a funnel.

It uses plain, concrete language that a guy who works with his hands would understand just as easily as a guy who runs a company. No abstractions. No words that require a psychology degree to decode. If a sentence could appear in a self-help book or a wellness newsletter, rewrite it.

It doesn't moralize. It doesn't tell men what they should feel or do. It observes, names, and explains.

It uses profanity when it's earned. Not for shock value. Because some moments call for it and softening them would be dishonest.

## Voice: what it does not sound like

It doesn't sound like a marketer. It doesn't sound like a therapist. It doesn't sound like a life coach who's read too many books about mindset.

It doesn't over-explain. It doesn't hedge everything. It doesn't apologize for being direct.

It doesn't start sentences with "I think" or "Perhaps" or "It's worth noting that." It says the thing.

It doesn't validate the reader before saying the thing. No "It makes sense that you feel this way" as a warm-up. No reassurance offered as a toll before the point gets made. Warmth in this writing comes from telling the truth plainly, not from cushioning it.

It doesn't hedge and then pivot. Constructions like "While it's true that X, it's important to remember Y" are a machine's way of having it both ways. Take a position. If two things are true at once, say both plainly and let the tension stand, the way the opening of this guide does.

It doesn't balance every claim into mush. Presenting both sides with equal weight on everything is a way of committing to nothing. This writing has a point of view. When a common belief is wrong, say it's wrong.

## Second person and staying inside the experience

Write in second person throughout. You, not he or they or men. Stay in second person for the entire post and don't drift into third person to describe the reader's experience from the outside. Second person keeps the reader from observing at a safe distance. He can't read it as being about someone else if every sentence is addressed directly to him.

Write from inside the experience, not from outside it looking in. Don't describe what a man feels. Put the reader inside the feeling before you explain it. Name the specific behavior first. Three concrete things he recognizes about himself. Then the insight. Not the other way around.

Say things once and move on. Don't restate a point in different words to make sure it landed. Don't qualify claims with phrases like "not in some vague way" or "in a very real sense" or "in a specific, measurable way." Make the claim and trust the reader to believe it.

Build to a drop. Long sentences that accumulate detail and pressure should land in a short one that carries the weight. "The engine stalls. And then it keeps stalling. And after a while it stops starting at all. Absolutely fucking empty." That rhythm is what makes writing hit rather than just inform.

## Language level

Write at a level anyone can follow. Short words over long ones. Concrete over abstract. Specific over general.

If you're about to write a word like "internalize," "process," "integrate," "regulate," or "dysregulate," stop and find a plain replacement.

Instead of "he is struggling to regulate his emotional responses," write "you snap at your kids and hate yourself for it afterward."

Instead of "internalized belief systems," write "the rules you've been running on your whole life without questioning them."

Instead of "maladaptive coping strategies," write "the things you do to get through the day that are making everything worse."

Instead of "unresolved grief," write "the thing you never dealt with that's still running the show."

The goal is that a man reading this on his phone at 11pm after a rough day can follow every sentence without having to slow down or translate.

No sentence fragments. Every sentence must be grammatically complete. "Not because you stopped caring." is not a sentence. Write it as part of the sentence it belongs to: "Not because you stopped caring, but because the person who made all of it matter is gone."

No abstract nouns that men don't actually use in conversation. Words like architecture, framework, structure, infrastructure, foundation, ecosystem, landscape, and similar abstractions make the writing sound like a consultant wrote it. Replace them with plain descriptions of what's actually happening. Instead of "the architecture that gave those things meaning," write "the person who made all of it matter" or "the reason any of it made sense."

Use contractions throughout. "That is" becomes "that's." "It is" becomes "it's." "You are" becomes "you're." "Do not" becomes "don't." "Cannot" becomes "can't." "Will not" becomes "won't." "They are" becomes "they're." "He is" becomes "he's." "Was not" becomes "wasn't." The only exception is when emphasis requires the full form. Writing without contractions sounds stiff and formal. These men don't talk that way and the writing shouldn't either.

## The ITC framework: use it invisibly or not at all

The Immunity to Change framework, competing commitments, Big Assumptions, and the four columns are never named in any post regardless of the content pillar.

The psychological insight those concepts contain should inform and shape what gets written the way background research shapes a good news story. The reporter doesn't cite the research. He tells you what it means.

So instead of writing "this is a competing commitment in action," write about what you're actually doing and why it makes sense that you can't stop doing it.

Instead of "your Big Assumption is preventing change," write about the belief you've been treating as a fact your whole life that's keeping you locked in place.

The one exception is the Map Explained content pillar, where the concepts behind the framework can be explained in plain descriptive language. Even there, the framework is never labeled as ITC or Immunity to Change. The ideas are explained as observations about how men get stuck, not as a named methodology.

## The program bridge: conditional, not automatic

The program bridge appears only in posts where the content pillar is Map Explained or Transformation Story. In those posts it's one quiet sentence at the very end of the post. It points without pitching. It doesn't use words like "journey," "ready," "next step," or "if you want to learn more."

For all other pillars, Raw Experience, Men and Grief, For the People Who Love Them, and The Stuck System, there's no program bridge. No mention of the Map, Phase 1, Phase 2, or Leading Through Loss. These posts stand completely on their own.

## Structural patterns

Posts open by putting the reader inside a specific, concrete moment or situation. Not a thesis. Not an explanation. A scene, a question, or a statement that names something he already recognizes about himself before he knows what the post is about.

Good example (from "The Loyalty Trap"):
"You're sitting at a table in a room dimly lit by a single lamp. It's close to midnight and everyone's long since gone to bed."

Good example (from "Asking for Help Sucks"):
"He's sitting alone on the couch, squinting in a pathetic attempt to bring the picture on the wall into focus."

The argument builds through observation and naming, not through explanation. Name the specific behavior first. Let him recognize himself. Then say what it means.

Section headings make an observation or a claim. They don't announce a topic. "You Lost More than a Person" is an observation. "Identity and Loss" is a topic label. The heading should do work on its own, not just label the work that follows.

Good examples from existing posts: "What Nobody Tells You." "The Decision You Don't Remember Making." "Facts Written in Blood." "When the Wheels Come Off."

Posts close quietly. They land a final thought and stop. No crescendo. No motivational push. No summary of what was just covered.

The mirrored conclusion is a specific violation of this rule. That's the move where the ending circles back to restate the opening image or idea with slight elevation, like tying a bow on the post. It feels polished and it reads as manufactured. The close should land a thought the post earned, not echo the thought it started with.

The same goes for unearned profundity. A post about one specific behavior should end on that behavior, not zoom out into a meditation on love, presence, or what it means to be alive. If the post earned a big ending through everything before it, take the ending. If it didn't, end small. Small and true beats big and hollow every time.

Good example (from "The Loyalty Trap"):
"Leaving your post isn't betrayal. It's your next act of love."

Good example (from "The Beliefs Grief Didn't Create"):
"It wasn't about grief magically lifting. It was seeing, for the first time, that what was keeping them stuck wasn't the loss itself. It was an old belief system that the loss turned all the way up. And that's something you can actually work with."

Sentence length must vary throughout the post. Long sentences that build an idea should be followed by shorter ones that land it. Strings of consecutive short sentences create choppiness, not rhythm. Save short sentences for moments that genuinely need them. When every sentence is short, none of them hit harder than the others.

## The frame underneath everything

The protection system keeping men stuck didn't start with the loss. The loss activated it. This is the core distinction and it must never be collapsed.

You're not broken. You're protected. The protection made sense at some point. It no longer serves you. But it can't be argued, willpowered, or pressed through on grit alone. It has to be understood first.

The Leading Through Loss Map is diagnostic, not transformational. Phase 1 shows a man what's been running the show. Phase 2 is where the unwinding begins. These aren't interchangeable and copy must never imply that understanding the problem alone fixes anything.

## Language the audience uses

Tired. Checked out. Going through the motions. Not myself. Stressed. Numb. Fine. I'm handling it. It is what it is.

## Language to avoid in every post

Grief journey. Healing journey. Sit with. Honor your grief. Give yourself grace. Process your emotions. Lean into. Show up for yourself. Self-care. Internalize. Regulate. Dysregulate. Integrate. Unpack. Deep dive. At the end of the day. Move the needle. Circle back.

Also avoid: "It's important to note." "Worth mentioning." "It's worth remembering." "One thing to consider." "In this space." "Do the work." "Not in some vague way." "In a very real sense." "In a specific, measurable way."

AI signature vocabulary, banned on sight: "delve," "tapestry," "navigate" (as a metaphor for living through something), "resonate," "elevate," "leverage," "robust," "profound" and "profoundly," "deeply" and "genuinely" and "truly" as intensifiers, "in today's fast-paced world" and every opener like it. These words mark the writing as machine-made to any reader who's spent time online in the last three years, and they violate the plain-language rule anyway.

## Titles and subtitles

The title is the first test. If it doesn't stop him, nothing else matters. A good title names something he already feels but hasn't said out loud, or makes a claim that's surprising enough to create a question he needs answered. It doesn't announce a topic. It creates a pull.

Good titles are specific and direct. They sound like something a real person would say, not something a content strategist would write. They don't use colons followed by generic subtitles. They don't use numbers unless the number is doing real work. They don't use words like "ultimate," "complete," "powerful," or "transformative."

The register to aim for, drawn from existing posts: "The Loyalty Trap." "When Fixing Things Breaks Everything." "Asking for Help Sucks." "The Beliefs Grief Didn't Create." These work because they're plain, specific, and slightly uncomfortable. They sound like something worth reading before you even know what they're about.

The subtitle functions as the Substack subtitle and the email preview line. It should add information the title withheld, not restate the title in different words. One sentence. No more than fifteen words. It should make the specific argument of the post, not describe the post in general terms.

Bad subtitle: "What men need to know about grief and moving forward."
Good subtitle: "Why staying stuck feels like loyalty and what it's actually costing you."

Both title and subtitle must be earned by the content. Don't write a title that promises more than the post delivers.

## Formatting

Short paragraphs. Often one to three sentences. White space isn't wasted.

Lists are used sparingly and when they appear they're punchy, not padded.

Bold is used occasionally for a phrase that needs to land harder. Not decoratively.

No bullet point summaries. No key takeaways sections. The writing is the takeaway.

No em-dashes. Use a comma, a period, or rewrite the sentence.
